Module 01
What is Phishing?
Phishing is a type of social engineering attack where criminals impersonate trusted entities to steal sensitive information like passwords, financial data, or personal details.
⚠️
Key Fact: In 2024, phishing attacks increased by 58.2% compared to the previous year, with AI-generated content making attacks harder to detect.
🎣
Types of Phishing
  • 📧
    Email Phishing — Mass emails mimicking banks, services, or employers asking you to "verify" your account.
  • 🎯
    Spear Phishing — Targeted attacks tailored to a specific individual using personal info from social media.
  • 🐋
    Whaling — High-value targets like CEOs or executives impersonated to authorize large transfers.
  • 📱
    Smishing — Phishing via SMS text messages with fraudulent links or phone numbers.
  • 📞
    Vishing — Voice phishing calls from fake "bank support" or "government agents".
  • 🔎
    Search Engine Phishing — Fake websites ranked on Google for terms like "bank login" or "PayPal support".
📊
Attack Lifecycle
  • 1️⃣
    Reconnaissance — Attacker researches target: name, email, employer, interests.
  • 2️⃣
    Weapon Creation — Crafts convincing email or website mimicking a trusted brand.
  • 3️⃣
    Delivery — Email, SMS, or social media message is sent to target(s).
  • 4️⃣
    Exploitation — Victim clicks link, opens attachment, or calls number.
  • 5️⃣
    Credential Capture — Victim enters data on fake site or malware is installed.
  • 6️⃣
    Data Exfiltration — Stolen data is sold or used for further attacks.

🚨 RED FLAG: Urgent language and all-caps creates panic to bypass rational thinking.
DATE: Mon, Jan 15, 2024 at 3:47 AM

Dear Valued Customer,

🚨 RED FLAG: Legitimate companies use your real name. Generic greetings signal mass phishing campaigns.

We have detected suspicious login activity from an unrecognized device in Russia (IP: 185.234.x.x).

🚨 RED FLAG: Fear-inducing fake details to make you act immediately without thinking.
Your account has been temporarily suspended for your protection.

You must verify your identity within 24 HOURS

🚨 RED FLAG: Artificial time pressure is a manipulation tactic. Real services don't give 24-hour ultimatums.
or your account will be permanently closed and all funds frozen.

Click the button below to verify your account:

✅ Verify My Account Now

🚨 RED FLAG: This button links to http://paypa1-support.ru/verify — a Russian fraudulent domain, not PayPal!

PayPal Security Team — paypal-secure-verify.com

🚨 RED FLAG: Fake footer domain. Real PayPal emails always link to paypal.com, never third-party domains.

8 RED FLAGS IN THIS EMAIL
01
Lookalike Domain
"paypa1-support.com" uses the number "1" instead of the letter "l" — a classic homograph attack.
02
Urgent/Threatening Language
"URGENT", "SUSPENDED", "NOW", "24 HOURS" — artificial pressure to make you skip verification steps.
03
Generic Greeting
"Valued Customer" instead of your name. Legitimate services personalize all emails.
04
Suspicious Sender Details
Email from a third-party domain, sent at 3:47 AM — not from PayPal's official servers.
05
Fear-Based Content
Mentions of Russia, specific IP addresses, and "funds frozen" to trigger panic response.
06
Suspicious Link Destination
Button links to a .ru domain, not paypal.com. Always hover before clicking to preview links.
07
Fake Security Footer
"paypal-secure-verify.com" is not a PayPal domain. Attackers add false legitimacy in footers.
08
No Official Branding Standards
Inconsistent fonts, colours, and lack of proper PayPal logo/design elements.
Module 03
Social Engineering
Attackers exploit human psychology rather than technical vulnerabilities. Understanding these tactics is your first line of defense.
🧠
The Human Firewall: 95% of cybersecurity breaches are caused by human error. Technology alone cannot stop social engineering — awareness is the key.
😨
Fear & Urgency
Creating panic to bypass rational decision-making
›
"Your bank account has been compromised! Click here immediately or all your funds will be frozen within 2 hours. This is your FINAL notice."
  • Legitimate organizations never give 1-2 hour ultimatums via email.
  • Take a breath — attackers want you to act before you think.
  • Call the organization directly using an official phone number from their website.
  • Real urgency would involve a direct phone call, not just an email.
🤝
Authority & Impersonation
Pretending to be a boss, IT dept, or government agency
›
"Hi, this is John from IT. We've detected malware on your computer. I need you to install this remote access tool and provide your login credentials to begin the cleanup."
  • IT departments never ask for your password — they can reset it themselves.
  • Verify identity by calling back on an official internal number, not the one they provided.
  • Check email headers — an executive's real email vs. a lookalike domain.
  • Legitimate remote support uses company-approved tools, not random downloads.
🎁
Greed & Reward Lures
Enticing victims with prizes, free money, or exclusive deals
›
"Congratulations! You've been selected as our monthly winner for a $500 Amazon Gift Card. Claim your prize in the next 30 minutes before it expires. Just pay a $5 processing fee to unlock it."
  • You cannot win a contest you never entered. Full stop.
  • "Pay a small fee to claim your prize" is always a scam.
  • Legitimate companies don't email random people about prizes.
  • If it sounds too good to be true — it is.
💬
Pretexting
Fabricating an elaborate scenario to gain trust
›
"Hi, I'm calling from your company's HR department. We're doing a salary audit and need you to confirm your employee ID, date of birth, and home address for our records."
  • HR already has all your information — they would never need to "verify" it with you.
  • Always verify unexpected requests through official channels.
  • Be wary of callers who "already know some of your info" — this builds false trust.
  • Attackers research targets on LinkedIn/social media to craft believable scenarios.
🙏
Sympathy & Trust
Exploiting compassion — fake charities, stranded friend scams
›
"Hey, it's Sarah! I'm stranded in London after being robbed. My phone is almost dead. I need you to send $300 via wire transfer so I can get home. I'll pay you back tomorrow."
  • Call your friend directly on their real phone number to verify.
  • Disaster/emergency charity scams spike after real-world events (earthquakes, floods).
  • Ask personal questions only the real person would know the answer to.
  • Never wire money to someone you cannot positively verify.
🔄
Quid Pro Quo
Offering something in exchange for information or access
›
"We're offering a free one-year subscription to our VPN service for completing a 5-minute security survey. Just enter your work email, password hint, and company name below."
  • No legitimate service asks for password hints in a survey.
  • Free offers requiring sensitive data cost far more than advertised.
  • Attackers collect partial info across multiple interactions to build complete profiles.
  • Use a separate email for sign-ups to protect your primary address.

Spotting Fake Websites
Phishing websites can look pixel-perfect to legitimate ones. Use this URL checker tool and learn what to look for.
🔎
URL Safety Checker — Try it!
Enter a URL below to check for phishing indicators (try examples: paypa1.com, www.paypal.com, g00gle-login.net)
🚩
Warning Signs in URLs
  • ❌
    Misspellings: paypa1.com, g0ogle.com, micros0ft.com
  • ❌
    Added words: paypal-secure.com, amazon-support.net
  • ❌
    Wrong TLD: paypal.ru, apple.tk, bank.xyz
  • ❌
    Subdomain tricks: paypal.com.attacker.ru — "paypal.com" is just a subdomain here!
  • ❌
    IP addresses: http://192.168.1.50/login — never a legitimate bank URL
  • ❌
    HTTP only: No padlock icon means no encryption — never enter data here
  • ❌
    Unicode lookalikes: аpple.com (Cyrillic "а") looks identical to apple.com
✅
Signs of a Legitimate Website
  • ✅
    HTTPS + Padlock: Look for 🔒 in the address bar and "https://" prefix
  • ✅
    Correct domain: The domain directly before the first "/" should match the official brand
  • ✅
    EV Certificate: High-value sites show the company name in the address bar
  • ✅
    Check with WHOIS: Look up domain registration age — scam sites are often days old
  • ✅
    Bookmark trusted sites: Navigate directly instead of using email links for banking/shopping
  • ✅
    Password manager autofill: Your manager won't autofill on fake domains — use this as a check!
  • ✅
    Google Safe Browsing: Check URLs at safebrowsing.google.com
⚠️
Real-World Lookalike Domain Examples

g00gle.com→ zeros for 'o'

Module 05
Best Practices
Actionable defenses you can implement today to dramatically reduce your risk of falling victim to phishing attacks.
01
🔐
Enable Multi-Factor Authentication
MFA adds a second layer of protection. Even if attackers steal your password, they cannot access your account without the second factor (SMS, authenticator app, or hardware key). Enable it on all important accounts — especially email, banking, and work systems.
02
🔑
Use a Password Manager
Password managers generate and store unique, complex passwords for every site. Crucially, they only autofill on the exact domain they were saved for — they will NOT autofill on a fake site, acting as an additional phishing detector. Use Bitwarden, 1Password, or Dashlane.
03
🖱️
Hover Before You Click
Always hover your mouse over a link before clicking it. The destination URL appears in the bottom-left of your browser. If it doesn't match the displayed text or goes to an unexpected domain, do not click. On mobile, hold-press links to preview the URL.
04
✅
Verify Requests Independently
Received an urgent request from your bank, boss, or IT? Don't use contact details from the message. Instead, navigate directly to the official website or call a known number. This simple step prevents almost all impersonation attacks.
05
🔄
Keep Software Updated
Outdated browsers and operating systems have known vulnerabilities that phishing attacks exploit. Enable automatic updates for your OS, browser, and antivirus. Many phishing attacks deliver malware that exploits unpatched software silently.
06
🚨
Report Suspicious Emails
Never just delete phishing emails. Use your email client's "Report Phishing" or "Mark as Spam" button. For work emails, forward to your IT security team. Reporting helps protect everyone in your organization and trains spam filters to catch similar attacks.
07
🎓
Limit Your Digital Footprint
Attackers build profiles from LinkedIn, Facebook, and other public sources to craft targeted spear-phishing emails. Limit personal information on social media: avoid posting your work email, employer details, phone number, or vacation schedule publicly.
08
🧪
Don't Open Unexpected Attachments
Malicious attachments can execute code the moment you open them. PDFs, Word docs (.docm), Excel files (.xlsm), and .zip files are common delivery vehicles. If you weren't expecting a file — even from a known sender — verify via a separate channel before opening.
✅
STOP. THINK. CONNECT. Before acting on any digital communication — pause, consider if it's legitimate, and verify through a trusted channel if in doubt. This 10-second habit prevents most attacks.
